## What the problem asks

A "covering system" is a finite list of rules like "numbers that leave remainder 3 when divided by 8". Each rule has a divisor, called its modulus, and a remainder. The list covers if every whole number, positive or negative, obeys at least one rule. The question asks whether such a list exists when every modulus is one less than a prime number of at least 5 — so the allowed moduli are 4, 6, 10, 12, 16, 18, and so on. The moduli must all be different; reusing a modulus with a new remainder is not allowed. This problem is open on the published catalogue. Nobody knows the answer, yes or no.

## What we tried

One automated run in search-first mode, costing $12.84, which found nothing and produced no certified results. Three earlier attempts had all attacked the same candidate size and all timed out. After that run, a hand analysis costing nothing — no model calls, milliseconds of computation — replaced the search with an algebraic test. Every modulus in such a list must divide one number, the least common multiple, and only 29 values of that number below 400,000 are even arithmetically possible. The test asks, for each one, whether the rules could fit together at all, without ever choosing remainders.

## What we found

**Twenty of the 29 possible sizes are impossible.** The argument counts how much the rules overlap. Sort the whole numbers into groups by their remainder when divided by some number `d`. A rule whose modulus is a multiple of `d` sits inside a single group; every other rule spreads evenly across groups and cancels out when you weigh the groups against each other. So the imbalance between groups has to be paid for entirely out of the overlap — the amount by which the rules double-cover. When the arithmetic is tight, there is almost no overlap to spend, and one heavy rule cannot be balanced.

The two sizes that had consumed all five previous attempts, 55,440 and 100,800, both fail this way in under a millisecond. For 100,800 the overlap can be at most 9, while the imbalance is at least 1,903. Four solver runs had previously failed to settle these two, one of them running 37 minutes without an answer.

**So no such list exists with least common multiple below 110,880** — and that is now settled by proof rather than by a filter, which is what the previous record at 55,440 was.

The test is also provably finished. Its tightest form can be decided completely, without trying combinations, and on the nine remaining sizes it rules out nothing at all: those sizes have loose arithmetic, leaving thousands of units of overlap to absorb any imbalance. The nearest miss, 302,400, falls short by 1,944. This is not a matter of searching harder.

The same test was checked against 3,130 genuine covering systems built by hand and never wrongly rejected one, and the two closest calls were recomputed in exact fractions rather than decimals.

## What would move it

Only a witness, or an argument that works for all sizes at once. The nine remaining candidates need a satisfiability solver — software that decides whether a set of constraints can be met — and they are harder targets than the ones already tried, being both larger and looser. The list of 29 stops at 400,000 because that is where the computation stopped, not because of any theorem, so a covering system could exist at a far larger size. **This work removed candidates; it did not answer the question, and it made the remaining ones look worse rather than better.**
