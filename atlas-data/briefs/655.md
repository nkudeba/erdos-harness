## What the problem asks

Take n points on a flat plane. They must follow one rule: if you draw a circle centred on any one of the points, that circle passes through at most two of the other points. Now measure the distance between every pair of points and count how many different lengths appear. The question asks whether that count is always noticeably more than half of n. "Noticeably more" means at least (1+c) times n/2, for some fixed positive number c, once n is large enough. In plain terms, the question is whether the rule forces many different distances, beyond a basic minimum.

## What we found

Lean accepted a complete proof that the formal version, as written, is false. The proof relies on no unproven assumptions. The example is the regular polygon: n points spaced evenly around a circle, for any n of 3 or more. These points obey the rule, because a circle centred at one of them crosses the polygon's circle in at most two places. Yet they produce only about n/2 different distances, since opposite steps around the polygon give equal lengths. A second check shows every set that obeys the rule has at least about (n−1)/2 distances. These two supporting steps were written down in Lean, but they were not proved there separately. This is not a solution of the published problem.
