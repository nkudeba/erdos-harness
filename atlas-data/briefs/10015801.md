## What the problem asks

Take an endless list of whole numbers, called A. It obeys one rule: any target number n can be written as a sum a + b of two list members (with a no larger than b) in at most two ways. Now count how many list members lie below a cutoff N, and divide that count by the square root of N. The claim is about how small this ratio gets. As N grows, it must keep dipping back down, again and again, to about 2.0804 or lower. That number is the square root of 3 divided by the natural logarithm of 2. In other words, such a list cannot stay crowded forever.

## What we found

The problem is **not solved**. Seven supporting statements were recorded as certified. They are side results, not the claim itself. Nine hypotheses are still open. The most useful finding concerns any finite piece of the list. Each sum can arise from at most 4 ordered pairs. From this, a measure of how often differences between members repeat, called "energy", is at most 3 times the square of the piece's size. The proof plan needs this as its only fact about counting. Everything after it is analysis. A simpler count shows the ratio always stays below about 2.83. That is short of 2.08, so the harder argument is truly needed. The Lean versions of these facts are written down but not yet proved.
