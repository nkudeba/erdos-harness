## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes. This is Erdős Problem #403 on erdosproblems.com, which traces back to Burr and Erdős. I believe it is marked as solved in the affirmative: only finitely many solutions exist. My memory is that Frankl and Lin (c. 1976) settled it, showing that the largest solution is 2⁷ = 2! + 3! + 5!. I am moderately confident (about 60–70%) about the attribution and the year, and fairly confident that the problem is regarded as solved.

   Two points about the Lean encoding:
   - Because ℕ includes 0 and 0! = 1! = 1, the formal set has small solutions such as 2 = 0! + 1!, 4 = 0! + 1! + 2! and 8 = 0! + 1! + 3!.
   - It also has m = 0 with {0} or {1}.

   None of this affects finiteness.

2. **Strategy (proposing now, not remembered in detail).** The idea is to play the 2-adic valuation against size.
   - If 2^m = Σ aᵢ!, then a_k! ≤ 2^m < e·a_k!, so m ≈ log₂(a_k!), which is roughly a_k·log₂ a_k.
   - Meanwhile v₂(aᵢ!) = aᵢ − s₂(aᵢ) < a_k for every term.
   - So the sum must vanish modulo 2^m, far beyond each term's own 2-adic valuation. This forces massive carry cancellation among the binary expansions of the factorials.
   - One would argue that the smallest terms fix the low-order bits. Then, working modulo successively larger powers of 2, each additional factorial is forced, and the forced chain terminates with no valid continuation beyond a small bound.
   - The remaining small cases would be finished by finite computation, e.g. `decide` or `norm_num` over a bounded range.

   I do not remember the exact mechanism Lin used. The details above are my reconstruction, not a recalled proof.

3. **Known proofs.** I only vaguely recall a published proof, attributed to Lin / Frankl–Lin around 1976, and cannot give a precise reference. I do not know of any formal Lean proof of this statement.
