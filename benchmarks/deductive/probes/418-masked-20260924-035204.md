## Answer

**1. Recognition**

Yes. This is the question of whether there are infinitely many **noncototients**, meaning positive integers not of the form n − φ(n).

- **Origin.** Erdős and Sierpiński asked it. I believe it is catalogued as Erdős Problem #418 on erdosproblems.com. I'm fairly but not fully confident of that number. I don't recognise the label "58109".
- **Resolution.** Browkin and Schinzel settled it affirmatively (J. Browkin, A. Schinzel, "On integers not of the form n − φ(n)", Colloq. Math., 1995). They showed that 2^k · 509203 is a noncototient for every k ≥ 1.
- **Later work.** Flammenkamp and Luca (around 2000) found further infinite families of this type.

I'm highly confident about the problem and the Browkin–Schinzel resolution. I'm moderately confident about the exact constant 509203 and the problem number.

**2. Strategy (remembered in outline, details reconstructed)**

I would follow Browkin–Schinzel and show that m = 2^k · 509203 is never n − φ(n).

- **Parity of n.** Since m is even, n − φ(n) even forces n to be even or n odd with a particular structure. Casework on the prime factorisation of n handles this.
- **Few-prime-factor cases.** When n has few prime factors, for example n = 2^a·p or n = p^a, n − φ(n) = m reduces to requiring a number of the shape 509203·2^j ± 1 to be prime. That is ruled out by a covering system of congruences. 509203 is a Riesel number, whose covering primes are {3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 241}.
- **Many-prime-factor cases.** When n has many prime factors, size and 2-adic valuation arguments on n − φ(n) exclude them.

I remember the main idea (a covering-congruence number times powers of 2). The precise case analysis is my own reconstruction rather than recalled detail.

**3. Formal proof**

I don't know of any Lean or Mathlib formalisation. A formal proof would need to do two things:

- verify the covering congruences, which is decidable and finite;
- carry out the factorisation casework on n, which is the harder part.

On the Lean side, n = 0 and n = 1 both give 0, which the condition 0 < m excludes. So the formal statement matches the informal one.
