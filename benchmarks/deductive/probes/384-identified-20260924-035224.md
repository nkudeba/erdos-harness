## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes. This is Erdős problem #384 on erdosproblems.com. The underlying result is due to **E. F. Ecklund Jr., "On prime divisors of the binomial coefficient," Pacific J. Math. 29 (1969)**. It states that for n ≥ 2k > 2, C(n,k) has a prime factor p ≤ n/2, with the single exception C(7,3) = 35. I'm fairly confident, around 80–85%, about the attribution and that the site lists it as solved by Ecklund.

   The Lean statement uses `2 * p ≤ n`, i.e. p ≤ n/2. That matches Ecklund's theorem, not the strict "p < n/2" in the informal text. The strict version is actually false for C(6,2) = 15 = 3·5, since the only prime below 3 is 2, and 2 does not divide 15. So the formalization is the correct, provable version. With k and n−k both covered and the n = 7 cases excluded, it looks faithful.

2. **Strategy (proposed now, loosely modelled on what I recall).** I remember the result, not the details of Ecklund's proof, so this is a reconstruction.
   - Reduce by symmetry to 2 ≤ k ≤ n/2, and suppose no prime p ≤ n/2 divides C(n,k).
   - Then every prime factor of C(n,k) lies in (n/2, n]. Each such prime occurs to exponent exactly 1, and it must be one of n, n−1, …, n−k+1.
   - This gives C(n,k) ≤ ∏ of the primes in (max(n/2, n−k), n]. That is at most n^r, where r is the number of primes among k consecutive integers, roughly ≤ k/2 + 1 or better with a sieve bound.
   - Compare with the lower bound C(n,k) ≥ (n/k)^k. Also use Kummer's theorem: no carries when adding k and n−k in base p for all small p, including p = 2. This forces strong digit constraints and kills most cases.
   - Finish with a finite computer or `decide` check for small n. In Lean that last step would be the heavy part.

3. **Known proofs.** The published proof I know of is Ecklund 1969 in Pacific J. Math. Related later work is by Ecklund–Eggleton–Erdős–Selfridge on prime factors of binomial coefficients. I do not recall any specific existing Lean or Mathlib formal proof of this statement.
