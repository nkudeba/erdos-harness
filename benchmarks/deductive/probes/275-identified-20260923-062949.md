## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes. This is Erdős Problem #275 on erdosproblems.com, a question of Erdős about covering systems. My recollection is that it is marked as solved there. The proof I associate with it is:

   - R. B. Crittenden and C. L. Vanden Eynden, "Any n arithmetic progressions covering the first 2ⁿ integers cover all integers," *Proc. Amer. Math. Soc.* (1970). A 1969 announcement may also exist.

   My confidence is about 80% that the problem is solved and that it was solved by Crittenden–Vanden Eynden, and about 60% on the exact year and venue.

   The bound 2ʳ is sharp. The progressions 2^{i−1} mod 2^i for i = 1…r cover 1, …, 2ʳ−1 but miss 0 mod 2ʳ.

   I believe the Lean statement matches the informal one faithfully. Taking the moduli as ℕ with nᵢ > 0 is harmless. For r = 0 the hypothesis would require covering k = 0 with no congruences, which is impossible, so the r = 0 case is vacuously consistent.

2. **Strategy.** This is a proposal of mine, not a proof I remember in detail.

   - Argue by contradiction: suppose some x is uncovered, and translate so that the block of 2ʳ consecutive integers is [0, 2ʳ).
   - Encode each non-covered residue by a polynomial or group-ring factor over roots of unity. One candidate is ∏ᵢ (1 − ζ_{nᵢ}^{k − aᵢ}), or an indicator product over ℤ/L with L = lcm(nᵢ).
   - Show that the resulting function of k is a "low-degree" combination of at most 2ʳ exponential characters. This comes from expanding the product into 2ʳ terms, each of the form a constant times ω^k for a root of unity ω.
   - A nonzero linear combination of at most 2ʳ distinct geometric sequences ω^k cannot vanish at 2ʳ consecutive integers, by a Vandermonde argument. So vanishing on the block would force the function to vanish identically, contradicting the uncovered x.
   - The delicate point is that the naive product is not literally the covering indicator. The real proof likely needs a more careful construction or an induction on r, splitting on one modulus.
