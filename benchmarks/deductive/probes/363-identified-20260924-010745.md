## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes. This is Erdős Problem #363 on erdosproblems.com. It is in the circle of questions around the Erdős–Selfridge theorem (1975), which says a product of two or more consecutive positive integers is never a perfect power. That theorem rules out the case n = 1.
   - I believe the problem is answered in the negative: there are infinitely many such collections. That matches the Lean statement, which asserts `Set.Infinite`.
   - I recall Ulas (around 2005, "On products of disjoint blocks of consecutive integers") giving infinite families for blocks of length 4 with suitable n. I believe he also had an explicit counterexample to the original question.
   - I recall Bauer and Bennett (around 2007) extending this, and I think Bennett and van Luijk (around 2012) handled blocks of length 5 and some other cases.
   - I am fairly confident (~80%) that the problem is catalogued as disproved and that Ulas's work is the key reference. I am less confident (~50%) about the exact parameters (block lengths, number of blocks) each paper covers.

2. **Strategy.** I would give an explicit parametric family and check it. The starting point is the identity m(m+1)(m+2)(m+3) = (m² + 3m + 1)² − 1. With it, each length-4 block becomes a value of X² − 1.
   - Next, find polynomial parameters a(t), b(t), … so that the product of several such values is a square of a polynomial times a square. Equivalently, find points on a Pell-type conic or elliptic curve with infinitely many rational or integer points.
   - Then check the side conditions for infinitely many t: the intervals are disjoint, they avoid 0, and they have length at least 4. Distinct t must also give distinct collections.
