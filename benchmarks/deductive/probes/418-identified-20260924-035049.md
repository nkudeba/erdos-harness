## Answer

**1. Recognition**

Yes. This is Erdős Problem #418 on erdosproblems.com. It asks whether infinitely many positive integers are *noncototients*, i.e. never of the form n − φ(n).

To my knowledge it is solved in the affirmative by J. Browkin and A. Schinzel, "On integers not of the form n − φ(n)", Colloquium Mathematicum, around 1995. They showed that 2^k · 509203 is a noncototient for every k ≥ 1.

- I am fairly confident about the problem and the Browkin–Schinzel resolution, roughly 80–85%.
- I am less sure about the exact year and about whether the result is stated for all k ≥ 1.

The Lean statement matches the question. Nat subtraction is harmless here because φ(n) ≤ n, and n = 0 gives 0, which the condition 0 < m excludes.

**2. Strategy**

This is partly remembered (the family 2^k · 509203 and its link to Riesel numbers) and partly reconstructed now.

- Take m = 2^k · 509203. The number 509203 is a Riesel number: 509203 · 2^j − 1 is composite for every j, because a finite covering set of primes always supplies a divisor.
- If n = 2^a · p with p an odd prime, then n − φ(n) = 2^(a−1)(p + 1). Setting this equal to m forces p = 509203 · 2^(k−a+1) − 1, which is composite by the covering property. So n cannot have this shape.
- The remaining shapes of n must then be excluded:
  - n prime gives n − φ(n) = 1.
  - Odd n and more complicated even n need parity and size arguments.
  - One such bound is n − φ(n) ≥ √n for composite n.
  - Some case analysis on the factorisation of n is needed.

I don't remember how Browkin–Schinzel handle the general case, so that part is my own proposal and may be incomplete.

For a formalization, the covering congruences can be checked by `decide` or `norm_num` on residues. The general-n case analysis is the hard part.
