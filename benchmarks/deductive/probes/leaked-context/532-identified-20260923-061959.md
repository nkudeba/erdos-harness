## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes, with high confidence (~99%). This is **Hindman's theorem**, also called the finite sums theorem. It was proved by Neil Hindman in "Finite sums from sequences within cells of a partition of ℕ" (J. Combin. Theory Ser. A, 1974). It is catalogued as Erdős problem #532, listed as solved. Graham and Rothschild had asked it, and Erdős posed it too. The statement here is the 2-colour case with the set-based formulation: an infinite set A of positive naturals whose non-empty finite subset sums all get one colour.

2. **Strategy.** I would use the **Galvin–Glazer ultrafilter proof**, which I remember from sources such as Hindman–Strauss, *Algebra in the Stone–Čech Compactification*, and Mathlib's own formalisation. It is not my invention.
   - βS is a compact right-topological semigroup, so by Ellis–Numakura it has an idempotent ultrafilter p = p + p.
   - I would take S = ℕ+ under addition, or restrict to non-principal ultrafilters on ℕ. This avoids the trivial idempotent at 0 and gives positivity.
   - One colour class C lies in p.
   - Recursively pick x₁ < x₂ < …. At each step, shrink C to C* = {x ∈ C : C − x ∈ p} and intersect with translates. Every finite sum of the chosen terms then lands in C.
   - Choosing the xₖ strictly increasing makes them distinct. Then A = {xₖ} is infinite, and subsets of A correspond exactly to sub-sums of the sequence.

   In Lean, the realistic plan is not to re-derive this. Instead:
   - Invoke Mathlib's `Mathlib/Combinatorics/Hindman.lean`. It defines `Hindman.FS` over a stream and proves a finite-cover/partition-regularity theorem, via `Ultrafilter.exists_idempotent_of_compact_t2_of_continuous_left`. I don't reliably remember the exact theorem name and would `#check`/search it.
   - Glue the stream version to the set version. This needs a stream of distinct positive terms, e.g. by working in ℕ+ or by extracting a strictly increasing subsequence.
   - Show that every finite subset of the range is a sum realised in `FS`.

   The glue is where the real formalisation work lies.

3. **Known proofs.**
   - Published: Hindman (1974), a combinatorial proof. Also Baumgartner's short combinatorial proof (JCTA, 1974), and the Galvin–Glazer ultrafilter proof, circulated around 1975 and presented in Comfort's 1977 survey and in Hindman–Strauss.
   - Formal: Mathlib contains Hindman's theorem in the stream/`FS` form, in `Mathlib/Combinatorics/Hindman.lean`, by David Wärn.
