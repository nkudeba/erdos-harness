## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes, with high confidence (about 95%). This is the **Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture** on restricted sumsets. Erdős and Heilbronn posed it in the 1960s, and it is catalogued in Erdős–Graham's problem book. It was proved by **J. A. Dias da Silva and Y. O. Hamidoune** in 1994 (Bull. London Math. Soc.), using exterior algebra and representation theory of the symmetric group. **Alon, Nathanson and Ruzsa** (Amer. Math. Monthly 1995; J. Number Theory 1996) gave a much simpler proof by the polynomial method.

   On the Lean statement: the small cases are harmless. For |A| ≤ 1 the left side is negative in ℤ, so they hold trivially. The case |A| = 2 is an exact equality, and the empty set is not an issue. As far as I can see, the statement matches the published theorem.

2. **Strategy I would try first.** This is the Alon–Nathanson–Ruzsa argument via Alon's **Combinatorial Nullstellensatz**, and I remember it from those sources rather than proposing it now.
   - Let k = |A|. First handle the case 2k − 3 ≥ p by shrinking A, since restricted sumsets are monotone in A.
   - Otherwise, suppose |A∔A| ≤ 2k − 4 and choose a set C of size exactly 2k − 4 containing A∔A.
   - Consider the polynomial f(x, y) = (x − y) · ∏_{c∈C}(x + y − c). It vanishes on all of A × A.
   - Its degree is 2k − 3. The coefficient of x^{k−1} y^{k−2} is C(2k−4, k−2) − C(2k−4, k−1) = C(2k−4, k−2)/(k−1), up to sign. This is nonzero mod p because 2k − 4 < p.
   - Since |A| > k − 1 and |A| > k − 2, the Nullstellensatz then gives a contradiction.
