## Answer

**1. Recognition.**
Yes — I recognise this as the Crittenden–Vanden Eynden theorem/conjecture: "any $n$ arithmetic progressions covering the first $2^n$ integers cover all integers." I associate it with R. B. Crittenden and C. L. Vanden Eynden, around 1969–1970 (a *Proc. Amer. Math. Soc.* paper with essentially that title, plus an earlier *Bull. AMS* announcement), and it is catalogued as Erdős problem #275 on erdosproblems.com. My confidence in the attribution and the statement is moderate-to-high (~75%); my confidence in the finer history is low — I have a genuine but unreliable memory that the original proof was regarded as incomplete or hard to verify and that a complete/simplified proof appeared much later (possibly in the 2000s–2010s), and I cannot name that author. The constant $2^r$ is sharp, witnessed by the binary family $0 \bmod 2,\ 1 \bmod 4,\ 3 \bmod 8,\dots$, which covers $2^r-1$ consecutive integers but not all.

**2. Strategy I would try first.**
Induction on $r$, driven by the extremal binary example, which is what makes the constant a power of two. If some modulus is even, then each congruence with even modulus lies entirely inside one residue class mod 2; pick the class mod $2$ containing the fewest of them, so at least one congruence is discarded, substitute $x = c + 2y$ (a congruence $a_i \bmod n_i$ restricted to that class becomes a congruence in $y$ with modulus $n_i/\gcd(n_i,2)$), and observe that the length-$2^r$ interval becomes a length-$2^{r-1}$ interval for a system of at most $r-1$ congruences — then apply the induction hypothesis to conclude that class is covered entirely, and repeat for the other class. The residual difficulty, and where I expect the real content to sit, is the case where all moduli are odd (or more generally when the prime $p$ one must use is $\ge 3$): the naive count loses a factor $p$ in interval length while gaining only the congruences that live off the chosen class, so one needs the extra structural fact that a prime $p$ dividing the lcm must be "used" by at least $p$ congruences, or an argument that a class mod $p$ meeting no $p$-divisible congruence forces a smaller system to cover a full residue class. This strategy is **one I am proposing now**, reverse-engineered from the sharp example; I do **not** remember the actual argument of the published proof. In Lean the awkward part is the change of variables and the strengthened induction statement (one should induct on "covers $2^r$ consecutive terms of an arithmetic progression", not on intervals of $\mathbb{Z}$).
