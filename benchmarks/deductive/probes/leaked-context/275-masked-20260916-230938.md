## Answer

## 1. Recognition

Yes — with reasonably high confidence. This is the **Crittenden–Vanden Eynden theorem**: *if m arithmetic progressions (residue classes) cover 2^m consecutive integers, they cover all the integers.* The source I remember is

> R. B. Crittenden and C. L. Vanden Eynden, *"Any n arithmetic progressions covering the first 2ⁿ integers cover all integers"*, Proc. Amer. Math. Soc. **24** (1970), 475–481.

I'm ~85% confident on the attribution and the title, somewhat less on the exact year/volume. They also stated the stronger conjecture that m classes covering the first k·2^m integers cover all but at most … (a "k uncovered classes" version), which I believe was settled later by other authors — I do **not** remember reliably who or when, so treat that as unverified. The bound 2^m is known to be sharp (the classes 2^{i}·(something) … the standard extremal family uses moduli 2, 4, 8, …, 2^m). It is catalogued on erdosproblems.com among the covering-system problems, but I do not remember the problem number, and the id `80317` here is a harness id, not an Erdős number.

I want to flag one thing honestly: this is **not** an easy exercise despite how it reads. The original paper is several pages, and the naive inductions do not close.

## 2. Proof strategy I would try first

I would prove the contrapositive in a form strong enough to induct: *if m classes do not cover ℤ, then every block of 2^m consecutive integers contains an uncovered integer.* Let p^a be the largest power of a prime p dividing lcm(q_j), and split the classes into those with p^a ∣ q_j and the rest; the former occupy at most s distinct residues mod p^a and, crucially, all of them are constrained to lie in a single class mod p^{a-1} once you fix the finer structure, so one can select a residue r mod p^a that kills all s of them and restrict attention to the progression r + p^a·ℤ. On that progression the remaining m − s classes induce m − s classes in a rescaled copy of ℤ, and an interval of length 2^m meets the progression in ≥ 2^m/p^a terms; the induction closes provided p^a ≥ 2^s, which is the delicate bookkeeping step and the place where I expect the argument to actually be hard (and where, in my memory, the real paper does substantially more work — probably a careful analysis of how many classes can share the top prime power, plus an induction on the number of distinct prime powers rather than on m directly).

I am **proposing** this route now from the shape of the 2^m bound; I do not remember Crittenden–Vanden Eynden's actual argument. The sharp examples with moduli 2, 4, …, 2^m strongly suggest the "one prime power at a time, halving the interval" shape is right, but I would not bet that the naive version closes — my honest expectation is that it does not, and that formalizing this is a research-level task, not a tactic-golf task. (Also note the Lean statement is vacuous for m = 0: `Fin 0` is empty, so the hypothesis with a block of 2^0 = 1 integer is false.)
