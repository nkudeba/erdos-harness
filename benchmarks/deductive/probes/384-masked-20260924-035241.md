## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes, fairly confidently. This is a classical result of Erdős from the 1950s. The statement is that for 2 ≤ k ≤ n − 2, C(n, k) has a prime factor p ≤ n/2, except for C(7, 3) = C(7, 4) = 35. I believe it appears as a remark in Erdős's paper on Sylvester's theorem (Sylvester–Schur), around 1955.
   - E. F. Ecklund Jr. (Proc. AMS, 1969, "On prime divisors of the binomial coefficient") proved a stronger version. For n ≥ 2k > 2, C(n, k) has a prime factor p ≤ n/k, with the sole exception C(7, 3).
   - I am confident about the result and the exception. I am only moderately confident (~60–70%) about the exact Erdős citation and year.
   - I do not recognise the number "29873"; it looks like a harness or database label.

2. **Strategy.**
   - Use symmetry to reduce to 2 ≤ k ≤ n/2.
   - Suppose no prime p ≤ n/2 divides C(n, k). Then every prime factor of C(n, k) exceeds n/2.
   - Such a prime appears in n! exactly once, and to divide C(n, k) it must lie in (n − k, n]. So C(n, k) is at most the product of the primes in (n − k, n], each to the first power.
   - Among k consecutive integers, at most about ⌈k/2⌉ are odd, and fewer are prime (use a mod-6 count). This gives C(n, k) ≤ n^{≈k/2} or smaller.
   - Compare with the lower bound C(n, k) ≥ (n/k)^k. This gives a contradiction except for finitely many small (n, k), which are checked by computation; only (7, 3) survives.
   - The p = 2 constraint (by Kummer, k must be a binary submask of n) can also prune cases.
   - This is a strategy I am proposing now, loosely informed by memory of the Erdős/Ecklund style of argument. I am not reproducing a remembered proof.

3. **Known proofs.** For published proofs, see Ecklund (1969), which implies this statement, and Erdős's original note. I do not know of a formal Lean or Mathlib proof of this statement.
