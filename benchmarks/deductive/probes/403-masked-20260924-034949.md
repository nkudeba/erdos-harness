## Answer

**1. Recognition.** Yes, I think so, with moderate confidence (about 60–70%) in the attribution and lower confidence in the details.

- This looks like the Erdős question of whether only finitely many powers of 2 are sums of distinct factorials. I believe it is catalogued on erdosproblems.com, but I don't reliably recall the problem number.
- Small solutions include:
  - 1 = 0! or 1!
  - 2 = 2!, and also 0! + 1!
  - 3! + 2! = 8
  - 2! + 3! + 4! = 32
  - 2! + 3! + 5! = 128
- I recall a result, attributed to Shanzhen Lin (1980s), that 2^7 = 2! + 3! + 5! is the largest solution. I'm unsure of the exact reference and the year.
- One thing to check: the Lean statement allows 0 and 1 together in S (0! = 1! = 1). That still gives only finitely many pairs, so it should be harmless.

**2. Strategy.** This is a strategy I am proposing now, not one I remember from a source.

- For a solution with S nonempty, the 2-adic valuation of the sum is controlled by the smallest elements of S.
- Since 2^m has a huge valuation, the small factorials in S must nearly cancel modulo high powers of 2.
- More concretely, split S at a threshold n. The part with a ≥ n is divisible by 2^{v₂(n!)}. The part with a < n is a bounded quantity from a finite set, and it must be congruent to 2^m modulo 2^{v₂(n!)}.
- Combine this with congruences modulo odd primes (e.g. 3, 5, 7, since a! vanishes mod p for a ≥ p). This constrains m modulo the order of 2.
- The hope is to derive a contradiction for large max(S), probably via a finite computation.

I don't have a clean, complete argument in mind. My sense is that the known proof relies on a computational sieve rather than a slick argument. Formalizing it would likely need `decide`/`norm_num` over a large but finite case analysis.
