## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes. This is the two-colour case of **Hindman's theorem**, also called the Finite Sums Theorem.
   - It was proved by Neil Hindman in "Finite sums from sequences within cells of a partition of N" (J. Combin. Theory Ser. A, 1974).
   - It had been conjectured by Graham and Rothschild, and Erdős posed related questions.
   - I am very confident (>95%) that the mathematics is Hindman's theorem.
   - I do not recognise the catalogue number "73508". I cannot confirm which problem list or database that label refers to.

2. **Strategy I would try first.** This is the Galvin–Glazer ultrafilter proof, which I remember from standard sources such as Hindman–Strauss, *Algebra in the Stone–Čech Compactification*. I am not inventing it now.
   - Take a non-principal idempotent ultrafilter U on (ℕ, +). Such a U exists because βℕ \ ℕ is a compact right-topological semigroup, so the Ellis–Numakura lemma applies.
   - One of the two colour classes f⁻¹(k) lies in U.
   - Build a sequence recursively. Using idempotence (A ∈ U implies A* = {x ∈ A : A − x ∈ U} ∈ U), pick xₙ in the current set, then shrink the set to A* ∩ (A* − xₙ). Also intersect with {m > previous choices} and {m > 0}, which lie in U because U is non-principal.
   - This yields a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers, so B is infinite, and every non-empty finite sum of distinct terms lands in f⁻¹(k).
   - The Lean-specific point is to intersect with tail sets so the terms are distinct. The Finset sum over T ⊆ B then matches the finite-sum set of the sequence.
