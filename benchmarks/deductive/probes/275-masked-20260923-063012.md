## Answer

1. **Recognition.** Yes. This is the Erdős covering conjecture that "m arithmetic progressions covering 2^m consecutive integers cover all integers." It was proved by R. B. Crittenden and C. L. Vanden Eynden in "Any n arithmetic progressions covering the first 2^n integers cover all integers," Proc. Amer. Math. Soc. 24 (1970). An announcement appeared around 1969 in Bull. AMS. It is also discussed in Erdős-problem collections, and Zhi-Wei Sun has generalizations. I am fairly confident (~85%) about the attribution and rough dates, though not the exact page or volume details. The label "erdos_80317" does not match any catalogue number I recognize.

2. **Strategy.** I would use an exponential-polynomial / Vandermonde argument.
   - Suppose some integer z is uncovered. Define f(x) = ∏_j (1 − ζ_j^{x − u_j}) with ζ_j = e^{2πi/q_j}.
   - Then f vanishes at every covered integer, in particular on the whole block t, …, t+2^m−1, while f(z) ≠ 0.
   - Expanding the product writes f(x) as a sum of at most 2^m terms c_S·λ_S^x, where each λ_S is a nonzero root of unity. Equivalently, f satisfies a linear recurrence of order at most 2^m with nonzero constant term.
   - Such a sequence that vanishes on 2^m consecutive integers must vanish identically. You can see this either from invertibility of the Vandermonde matrix after merging equal λ_S, or by running the recurrence forwards and backwards. That contradicts f(z) ≠ 0.

   I believe this style of argument appears in the literature, but I am reconstructing it now rather than recalling a specific written proof. I believe Crittenden–Vanden Eynden's original proof was more combinatorial and longer. For Lean, the recurrence version is likely easier than working with a complex Vandermonde matrix.

3. **Known proofs.** I remember the published source above, but not the details of its proof. I do not know of any existing Lean or Mathlib formalization of this statement.
