## Answer

1. **Status.** As far as I know, this problem is still open. I do not recall any resolution.

2. **Expected answer (a guess).** I expect the answer is **yes**, based on the following heuristic.
   - Write k = log n. Every prime p ≤ k automatically divides some n+i, since the product covers k consecutive integers.
   - Only the primes p in (k, (2+ε)k] matter. For each such p, a "random" n misses p with probability about 1 − k/p, which is at most roughly 1/2 + O(ε).
   - Treating these primes as independent, the chance that n fails for none of them is about exp(−c·k/log k).
   - There are about e^k integers n with log n ≈ k. So the expected number of good n tends to infinity.

   This heuristic works for any fixed constant in place of 2+ε. So I expect the true answer is yes, probably with room to spare. The difficulty is making the heuristic rigorous. The Chinese remainder theorem alone cannot do it, because the product of the relevant primes is much larger than n. The problem is connected to Jacobsthal-function and sieve-type questions, where unconditional results are weak.
