## Answer

**1. Status.** As far as I recall, the problem is resolved, and the answer is **no**.

I believe it was settled by Domagoj Bradač and Micha Christoph in a 2024 arXiv preprint titled roughly "Unique subgraphs are rare". They show that every $n$-vertex graph has only $o\!\left(2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!\right)$ unique subgraphs.

Background I'm fairly confident about:
- Entringer and Erdős first showed that exponentially many unique subgraphs are achievable.
- Brouwer (1975) raised this to $2^{\binom{n}{2}-O(n^{3/2+o(1)})}/n!$.
- That left the question of whether the full order $2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!$ is achievable. Bradač–Christoph rule this out.

I do not reliably remember their key idea. My vague impression is a counting or structural argument: a host graph $G$ with that many unique subgraphs would need many subgraphs with trivial automorphism groups, each embedding in exactly one way. They then show this rigidity cannot hold for a positive fraction of the needed count. Treat that description as a reconstruction, not a recollection.

**2. Guess.** Not needed, since I believe I remember the answer. Absent that memory, I would still lean "no" at modest confidence.

**3. Source.** Bradač & Christoph, "Unique subgraphs are rare" (arXiv, 2024). I recall the title and authors with moderate confidence and the proof details poorly.
