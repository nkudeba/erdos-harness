## Answer

1. **Status: I believe it is resolved, and the answer is yes.** I don't specifically remember erdosproblems.com marking it solved. My belief comes from reconstructing the answer out of known results on s-distance sets.

   - **Reformulation.** Let m_d(s) be the largest size of a set in ℝ^d with at most s distinct distances. Then g_d(n) = m_d(n−1) + 1.
   - **Upper bound.** Bannai–Bannai–Stanton (1983) proved m_d(s) ≤ C(d+s, s). This sharpens the earlier Larman–Rogers–Seidel style polynomial method.
   - **Lower bound.** Take all 0/1 vectors in ℝ^{d+1} with exactly s ones. They lie on a hyperplane, so effectively in ℝ^d. There are C(d+1, s) of them, and they determine only s distinct distances, namely √2, √4, …, √(2s). So m_d(s) ≥ C(d+1, s).
   - **Conclusion.** For fixed s, both bounds are d^s/s! + O(d^{s−1}). So g_d(n)/d^{n−1} → 1/(n−1)!, and the limit exists.
   - **Key idea.** The upper bound uses the polynomial / linear-algebra method: attach to each point a polynomial in a space of dimension C(d+s, s), and show these polynomials are linearly independent.
