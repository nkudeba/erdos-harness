# 678

With M(n,k) = lcm(n+1, …, n+k), are there infinitely many triples m, n and k ≥ 3 with m ≥ n + k such that M(n,k) > M(m,k+1), that is, a block of k consecutive integers whose lcm beats that of a later block of k+1?

**Answer:** true

**Sources:** erdosproblems.com/678 (cached page); forum thread /forum/thread/678: Alexeev's comments (Jan 2026), which note that for a fixed k only finitely many (m, n) exist, and his Aristotle formalisation of Cambie's theorem, conditional on the prime number theorem; proof-claims page (empty); Cambie, arXiv:2410.09138 (Theorem 1 and its full proof in §2); pool.json (formal statement: for all large k there exist (m, n) with n + k ≤ m and the lcm of the (k+1)-block after m smaller than the lcm of the k-block after n).

**Key idea:** Compare each lcm with its block product through the excess ∏/lcm, which is fixed by residues modulo prime powers up to k. By the Chinese remainder theorem modulo M = lcm(1, …, k), Cambie takes a k-block starting at x with x ≡ 1 (mod m₀) and a (k+1)-block starting at y with y ≡ 0 (mod m₀), where m₀ is the part of M from primes ≤ √k. For each prime √k < p ≤ k he also fixes x mod p and y mod p, so that the two blocks contain the right numbers of multiples of p. Prime by prime this gives ∏(y..y+k)/lcm(y..y+k) = M·∏(x..x+k−1)/lcm(x..x+k−1), so lcm(x-block)/lcm(y-block) = M·∏(x-block)/∏(y-block) ≈ M/y. That exceeds any constant C once y ≈ M/(4C) and x is slightly smaller than y.

**Main steps:**
- Set M = lcm(1..k) and m₀ = ∏_{p≤√k} p^{⌊log_p k⌋}. Require x ≡ 1 and y ≡ 0 (mod m₀), and for each √k < p ≤ k require 1 ≤ x mod p ≤ p − (k mod p) and p − (k mod p) ≤ y mod p ≤ p.
- Check equality of the p-adic valuations of the two excesses for every prime. For p ≤ √k it follows from the matched residues mod m₀, since at most one term in the block exceeds the maximal power. For √k < p ≤ k, count multiples of p, since a window contains at most one multiple of p². Primes above k contribute nothing.
- Density claim (Claim 4): CRT solutions whose residues are restricted to large subsets for a few primes near k/2 (for y) or near k (for x) have no long gaps. With primes in the short intervals (k/2, (1+ε)k/2) and ((1−ε)k, k), this finds y in (M/(5C), M/(4C)) and x with x < y < x + M/(5Ck). The residues mod m₀ force y − x > k automatically.
- Compute lcm(x-block)/lcm(y-block) ≥ (M/(y+k))·(x/y)^k > 4C·(k/(k+1))^k > C.
- With C = 1, this gives the required (m, n) for every large k. For a fixed k there are only finitely many pairs, so k must be allowed to vary.

**A hit looks like:** the notes control each lcm through the ratio product/lcm and fix that ratio by CRT modulo all prime powers ≤ k, so that the later, longer block loses an extra factor of about lcm(1..k) relative to the earlier block. The two blocks are chosen close together and far below lcm(1..k).

**A partial hit looks like:** sporadic numerical examples (Selfridge's M(96,7) > M(104,8)); a heuristic that the lcm of consecutive integers can drop; a proof of finiteness for fixed k; or a CRT construction for one block that does not match the p-adic excess of both.

**Confidence:** high. I read the construction directly in Cambie's paper, and it is formalised in Lean, conditionally on the prime number theorem, which the density step uses.
