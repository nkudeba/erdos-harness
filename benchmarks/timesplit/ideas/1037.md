# 1037

If a graph on n vertices has every degree occurring at most twice and more than (1/2 + ε)n distinct degrees, must it contain a clique or an independent set much larger than log n?

**Answer:** false

**Sources:** erdosproblems.com/1037 (cached page); forum thread /forum/thread/1037: Sawhney's and Hunter's random-graph suggestions (13 Sep 2025), KoishiChan's copies idea and Cambie's "easy proof" (21–22 Sep 2025), Bloom's reading of the quantifiers, and Alexeev's note that Aristotle formalised the proof (Jan 2026); proof-claims page (empty); pool.json (formal statement).

**Key idea:** Start from a fixed graph G on n/4 vertices whose clique and independence numbers are O(log n), such as a typical sample of G(n/4, 1/2), and take four copies A, B, C, D. Join A and B completely, add nothing between C and D, and put a half graph between A∪B and C∪D. List each side by non-increasing degree and join v_i to w_j exactly when i + j ≤ n/2. The half graph adds strictly decreasing amounts to degrees that were already non-increasing, so degrees within A∪B are distinct, degrees within C∪D are distinct, and each value occurs at most twice. A degree count, using that at least half the vertices of G have degree at most about n/8 (after complementing G if needed), gives at least 3n/4 distinct degrees, and any clique or independent set meets each copy in a clique or independent set of G, so it has at most 4·O(log n) vertices.

**Main steps:**
- Fix a Ramsey-type graph G on n/4 vertices with ω(G) and α(G) of order log n. Complementing if necessary, assume at least half its vertices have degree ≤ (n+1)/8.
- Build H from the copies A, B, C, D: every edge between A and B, none between C and D, and the half graph v_i ~ w_j if and only if i + j ≤ n/2. Here v₁, …, v_{n/2} list A∪B and w₁, …, w_{n/2} list C∪D, each in non-increasing order of degree.
- Degrees are then strictly decreasing along each list, so every degree value occurs at most twice in H.
- Cambie's count shows at least 3n/4 distinct degrees, which is more than (1/2 + ε)n for any ε < 1/4.
- ω(H) ≤ 4·ω(G) and α(H) ≤ 4·α(G), so the largest trivial subgraph has O(log n) vertices. When 4 ∤ n, add a few near-universal vertices.

**A hit looks like:** the notes start from a random or Ramsey graph, whose largest clique and independent set are O(log n), and add structured edges between a bounded number of copies, typically a threshold (half) graph ordered by degree. This forces the degree conditions while clique and independence numbers grow by at most a constant factor.

**A partial hit looks like:** a probabilistic model with spread-out degrees, for example edge probabilities depending on vertex labels, that shows trivial subgraphs are O(log n) but does not meet "each degree at most twice" exactly; or an unfinished appeal to enumeration of random graphs with a prescribed degree sequence.

**Confidence:** high. The construction is explicit and elementary on the forum, and it is formalised in Lean. I did not re-verify the 3n/4 degree count; I took it from Cambie's post.
