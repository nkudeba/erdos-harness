## What the problem asks

Take a run of whole numbers, say the k numbers just after n: n+1, n+2, up to n+k. Their "least common multiple" is the smallest number that every one of them divides into evenly. Call it M(n,k). The question, from Erdős, is whether this value can ever repeat: if you slide the run far enough along the number line so the new run starts at or beyond n+k (that is, m ≥ n+k, so the two runs do not overlap), must the least common multiple always be different? The problem is listed as open on the problem catalogue, and remains open. Nothing here changes that.

## What we found

No repeat was found, and no proof was produced. The two easiest cases, k = 1 and k = 2 (runs of one or two numbers), are settled by elementary reasoning: there the least common multiple simply grows as you slide along, so it cannot repeat. The general case is untouched. A computer sweep found no example anywhere it looked; the recorded search bound is 1,000,000,000, and its recorded kind is exhaustive, meaning every case in that range was actually enumerated. For the k = 3 case the whole question collapses to one equation in two unknowns, and the only solution found in the swept range involves overlapping runs, which the statement permits. Repeats do occur, but only for runs that overlap by a small gap.

## What would move it

Two routes. To break the statement, someone needs one explicit triple of numbers (n, k, m) with the runs not overlapping and the two least common multiples equal. Our evidence says not to look by brute force: the swept ranges are empty and the structure found there suggests a bounded search is close to futile. To prove it, someone needs a general argument that works for every run length k at once. The known number-theory tools give results only for one fixed k at a time, and even k = 3 already turns into a hard equation-solving problem. Worth formalising meanwhile: the k ≤ 2 cases, a periodicity fact, and a smoothness fact. Those are side results, not the problem.
