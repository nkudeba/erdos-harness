## What the problem asks

A prime is a whole number above 1 that only 1 and itself divide evenly, such as 2, 3, 5 or 7. Take an endless list of primes in increasing order, q_1 < q_2 < …. A gap is the difference between one prime in the list and the next. The rule is that gaps never shrink: each gap is at least as big as the one before. The question is whether such a list must outgrow every fixed multiple of n², the square of a prime's position n in the list. Put another way: must q_n divided by n² eventually pass every number? The catalogue lists the problem as open. It also says no finite computation can settle it.

## What we found

The problem is not solved. The most useful finding is a simple bridge. Suppose the gaps never shrink. Take the gap at step k and multiply it by the number of steps from k up to n. That product is at most the distance from q_k to q_n. So suppose a list stayed below C·n² for some fixed number C. Then every gap in the first half of the list would be at most 2Cn. A counterexample would need about n/2 primes below C·n². Their gaps would have to never shrink and stay at most 2Cn. Two more facts were checked. After the first prime, every gap is even. Also, a gap e can repeat at most p − 1 times, where p is the smallest prime that does not divide e. For example, a gap not divisible by 3 appears at most twice.

## What would move it

One open claim remains, called "no short convex chain". A convex chain here means a run of primes whose gaps never shrink. The claim asks for an upper limit on how many such primes fit below C·n² when every gap is at most 2Cn. A separate screen, not part of this run, judged the answer likely "yes". It noted that gaps must grow without limit, because no endless list of primes has all gaps equal. It also informally reproved that q_n/n² stays above some positive number. That result is weaker than one by Richter in 1976. The screen says progress needs a proof that small gap values become rare. That would take a sieve, meaning a counting method that bounds long, nearly square-shaped patterns of primes.
