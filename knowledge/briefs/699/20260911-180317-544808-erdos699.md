## What the problem asks

Pick a whole number `n`. A "binomial coefficient" written C(n,i) counts the ways to choose i items out of n. Now pick two sizes i and j with 1 ≤ i < j, both at most half of n. The question asks whether the two counts C(n,i) and C(n,j) must always share a common prime factor — a prime number dividing both of them — that is at least as large as i. A prime is a whole number above 1 divisible only by 1 and itself. The conjecture says such a shared prime always exists. It is listed as open: nobody has proved it, and nobody has found a counterexample.

The formal Lean version commits to `p ≥ i`, not the stronger `p > i`; that choice is essential, because the stronger version is already known to be false. It also assumes n is quantified over all values, and that i ≥ 1 with i < j strictly — without those the statement would be trivially false or a different problem. So nothing here says anything about the stronger `p > i` variant.

## What we found

Two small Lean lemmas compiled with no gaps: one says n divided by its shared factor with j always divides C(n,j); the other is a divisibility step about consecutive numbers. Both are stepping stones, not progress on the hard part.

The most useful checked fact: if a prime p is bigger than i, then p divides C(n,i) exactly when the remainder of n on division by p is smaller than i. That turns a question about enormous numbers into a question about remainders. A consequence, also checked: any counterexample needs every prime factor of n, n−1, …, n−i+1 to be at most j — a severe restriction.
