## What the problem asks

Pick any whole number `n`. A "binomial coefficient" written C(n,i) counts the ways to choose `i` things out of `n`. The question takes two positions `i` and `j`, with `1 ≤ i < j` and `j` no more than half of `n`, and asks whether the two counts C(n,i) and C(n,j) must always share a prime factor — a prime number (a number divisible only by itself and 1) that divides both — where that shared prime is at least as large as `i`. The problem is listed as open. Nobody knows whether it is always true, and a single counterexample would settle it the other way.

## What we found

The most useful checked fact: if any prime sits in the window between `n-i` and `n`, that prime already divides both counts, and the statement holds for that `n` and every allowed `j`. So a counterexample can only occur when every one of the numbers `n-i+1` up to `n` is composite — that is, at the far end of a long stretch with no primes. Also checked: the case `i = 1` is always true by a short argument, so only `i` of 2 or more can fail. Separately recorded, not from this run: a published search covering roughly 4.17 × 10^19 candidate triples found nothing; that bound is third-party and was not rerun here.
