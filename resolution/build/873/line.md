=== GOAL

No line I can defend is likely to settle the problem. I am therefore choosing a major-progress line, and I say so here.

**Why no settling line.** The formal statement is equivalent to the Divisor Cluster Conjecture (DCC), uniformly in n:
- Packing gives DCC ⇒ the statement.
- The block construction gives "DCC fails at some θ₀" ⇒ the statement is false.
- The strict Lean form is equivalent to the ≪ form (frontier).
- k = 0 is excluded automatically, because the empty lcm is 1 and then F = ⊤ for X > 1.

So settling means proving uniform DCC for every θ < 1/2, or refuting it. That is a hyperbola analogue of Cilleruelo–Granville. No computation can finish it: PARI or SAT can only test finitely many n, and the statement is asymptotic in n.

**Target (Theorem A, uniform in the primes).** For every θ < 1/2, set δ = 1/2 − θ. For every n ≥ 1 and y > 0:

#{d | n : y ≤ d ≤ y(1+n^{θ−1/2})} ≤ 1 + (2^{240}·δ^{−14})^{ω(n)+1} + 2^{16/δ}.

The constants are to be fixed from the verified statement of the theorem used. The shape we need is K ≤ exp(C(θ)·(ω(n)+1)), with no dependence on which primes divide n.

**Corollary A′ (structure theorem for any disproof).** A cluster of K divisors at relative width n^{θ−1/2} forces ω(n) ≥ c(θ)·log K. So any counterexample to the problem must have window lcms with ω(L) → ∞ like log k.

**Stretch target (Theorem B).** Remove or weaken the ω-dependence, for example:
- make K depend only on θ and the rank of the group generated by the cluster's ratios, or
- make K depend only on θ and the number of primes dividing d_max/gcd(cluster).

**Why an expert would rate this 2, rising to 3 with B:**
- It is the first statement crossing the n^{1/4} curvature barrier for every θ < 1/2 on a class of n that is infinite and uniform in the primes. Today only ω = 2 is known, and ω = 3 is partial in the team's C1/C2/C4r.
- It turns the frontier's "a proof must use arithmetic" into an actual tool.
- B would be the first ω-free progress beyond 1/4.

=== WHY THIS LINE
The barriers say curvature stops at θ = 1/4. They also say anything beyond must use that all the points are divisors of the same n. The S-adic Subspace Theorem (quantitative Ridout) uses exactly that, and it never sees curvature. The elementary reduction is checked below.

**The reduction.** Let d_1 < d_j be in the cluster and g = gcd(d_1, d_j). Write u = d_j/g and v = d_1/g.
- u and v are coprime S-units for S = {p | n} ∪ {∞}.
- u ≤ n, and (u−v)/u = (d_j−d_1)/d_j ≤ n^{θ−1/2} ≤ u^{−δ}.

**Checking the subspace inequality for x = (u, v).** Use the forms X₁ − X₂ and X₂ at ∞, and the forms X₁ and X₂ at each p ∈ S.
- At the finite places the product is 1/(uv).
- At ∞ it is (u−v)v/u².
- The total is (u−v)/u³ ≤ u^{−2−δ} = H(x)^{−n−δ}, with n = 2.

In P¹ every proper subspace is a single point, so the number of distinct ratios d_j/d_1 is bounded by the number of subspaces for large solutions, plus the small solutions. Evertse's quantitative theorem gives about (2^{60n²}δ^{−7n})^{|S|} subspaces for H ≥ max(H(L), n^{4n/δ}) = 2^{8/δ}. The forms have height 1, so that threshold does not depend on the primes.

**Why the team's work points here.**
- Harness C1/C2/C4r (bounded ω via geometry of numbers) runs into the frontier's Baker barrier when the primes vary. The subspace route avoids absolute lower bounds, which is exactly what that barrier leaves open.
- It is consistent with the known lower bounds:
  - The polynomial families have ω(n) → ∞.
  - The examples {v, v+1} show that the pairwise ratio count cannot be uniform in |S|.
  - Erdős–Stewart–Tijdeman show unit-equation counts grow with s.
- Theorem B is where cluster structure, not pairs, has to enter. That is the honest frontier.

=== PITFALLS
- **No team work has been refereed yet.** Harness C1, the 1/700 saving and the polynomial families are model-refereed only. The Letendre-plus-packing exponent 1/4 is unverified. Do not build on any of these as settled.
- **Do not misquote the subspace theorem.** The "absolute" version cannot be uniform in |S| for this inequality: (v+1, v) satisfies it for every v. Any |S|-free bound must use more than pairs.
- **Circularity trap.** For A ⊆ {ω ≤ s}, a window's lcm has ω ≤ ks. Theorem A then needs k > C^{ks}, which is impossible. Do not claim the problem for bounded-ω sequences from Theorem A.
- **Barriers to avoid.**
  - Any curvature or Taylor argument stops at θ = 1/4.
  - Pairwise or Vandermonde inequalities stop at exponent 1/3.
  - Baker-type absolute bounds are useless when the primes vary.
  - Any proposed ω-free K must be at least exp(c/ε²) to survive the polynomial families.
- **Novelty.** Theorem A may be folklore. Check before calling it new: the #769 lesson was a Lean-verified result that turned out to be already known.
